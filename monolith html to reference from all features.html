PM Terminology — Reference Glossary

These are the standard terms used in professional project management. When you use these in meetings, reports, and communications, you signal expertise and command the room. Learn them. Use them consistently.

Project Sponsor Initiating
The individual or organization that provides the financial resources and formal authorization for the project. The Sponsor is the highest authority — they own the business need the project is solving.
Why it matters: Without a Sponsor, a project has no mandate. The Sponsor resolves escalated issues, approves scope changes, and is the person you ultimately report project success to.
Example: If a hotel chain is expanding a wing, the Regional Director of Operations who signed off on the budget is the Project Sponsor.
Stakeholders Governance
Any person, group, or organization that can affect, be affected by, or perceive themselves to be affected by the project. Stakeholders may have approval authority, veto power, or simply hold an interest in the outcome.
Why it matters: Unmanaged stakeholders become blockers. Identifying them early and managing their expectations is one of the most critical skills of a Project Manager.
Example: On a road construction project — the client, the utility company whose pipes run underground, the local residents, and the government inspector are all Stakeholders.
Resources Planning
Everything utilized to execute the project: human capital (labor and specialists), physical assets (machinery, vehicles, generators, tools), materials, budget, and time. In PM we do not refer to people as "just people" — labor is a resource with a cost, a capacity, and a schedule.
Why it matters: Resources are finite. Mismanaging them causes cost overruns and schedule delays. A PM tracks resource availability, utilization rates, and conflicts proactively.
Example: For a highway paving project — the asphalt crew (human resource), the paving machine (equipment resource), the asphalt material (material resource), and the allocated $2M budget (financial resource).
Requirements Planning
The conditions, capabilities, or items that must be present and satisfied for the project to begin, progress, or be accepted as complete. Requirements define what is needed — not what is wanted.
Why it matters: Unclear requirements are the primary cause of scope creep and rework. A PM documents requirements formally before mobilization to protect the project boundary.
Example: To begin a generator installation project — Requirements might include: approved electrical permit, generator unit on-site, concrete pad completed, and qualified electrician contracted.
Deliverable Execution
A tangible, verifiable work product or output produced by the project. Deliverables are what you physically hand over to the client or Sponsor at a defined point — they must be concrete and measurable, not vague.
Why it matters: Deliverables are how project success is defined and confirmed. If you cannot point to a deliverable, the work is not complete.
Example: "A completed 3-bedroom residential structure with electrical, plumbing, and roofing finishes, ready for occupancy inspection" — not just "a house."
Quantifiable Outcome Closing
The measurable benefit the Sponsor or organization realizes because the deliverable was produced. A Quantifiable Outcome is always expressed in numbers — percentages, dollar values, time saved, units produced, or capacity gained.
Why it matters: Deliverables are the output. Quantifiable Outcomes are the value. Sponsors fund projects for outcomes, not deliverables.
Example: A new warehouse facility (deliverable) enables the company to increase storage capacity by 40% and reduce logistics turnaround time by 2 days (quantifiable outcome).
KPI — Key Performance Indicator Monitoring
A specific, pre-agreed, measurable metric used to evaluate how successfully the project is performing against its objectives in real time. KPIs are not feelings — they are numbers tracked at regular intervals.
Why it matters: KPIs give the PM objective evidence of project health. They are the language used in board reports, sponsor meetings, and client reviews to prove performance without ambiguity.
Example: Schedule Performance Index (SPI) above 0.95 | Cost Variance within 5% of budget | Defect rate below 2 per 100 units | 95% of milestones hit on time.
Project Scope Planning
The defined and agreed boundaries of the project — specifying exactly what work IS included and, critically, what IS NOT included. Scope is formalized in the Project Charter and the WBS.
Why it matters: Scope creep (uncontrolled expansion of scope) is the number one cause of project failure. A locked scope with a formal change control process is non-negotiable.
Example: Scope includes: foundation, framing, roofing, electrical rough-in. Out of scope: landscaping, interior painting, and furniture installation.
Milestone Scheduling
A significant event or decision point in the project timeline that marks the completion of a major phase or achievement. Milestones have zero duration — they are checkpoints, not tasks.
Why it matters: Milestones are the anchors Sponsors and Stakeholders track. Missing a milestone is a formal trigger for a project status review.
Example: "Foundation poured and inspected — 15 Jul 2026" is a milestone. It signals Phase 1 is complete and Phase 2 can mobilize.
Predecessor Scheduling
A task that must be completed (or started) before another task can begin. Predecessors create logical dependency links between tasks and are the foundation of the Gantt timeline and critical path calculation.
Why it matters: If a predecessor slips, every task dependent on it is at risk. Tracking predecessors is how a PM identifies schedule impact before it becomes a crisis.
Example: "Install electrical conduit" (Task 8) cannot start until "Complete framing" (Task 5) is done. Task 5 is the Predecessor of Task 8.
Risk Risk Mgmt
An uncertain event or condition that has NOT yet occurred, but that — if it does occur — would have a positive or negative effect on project objectives. All risks are future-tense possibilities.
Why it matters: Identifying risks before they materialize gives the PM time to build mitigation strategies and avoid reactive, costly firefighting during execution.
Example: "There is a 40% probability that cement deliveries will be delayed due to supplier capacity constraints, which could push the foundation phase by 5 days."
Issue Risk Mgmt
A risk that has materialized and become a current, active problem requiring immediate resolution. Issues are present-tense — they are happening now and require an assigned owner and target resolution date.
Why it matters: The distinction between a Risk and an Issue is critical in PM reporting. An Issue demands immediate escalation and a concrete resolution plan, not just monitoring.
Example: "Cement delivery has been confirmed as delayed by 7 days. This is now a live Issue. Owner: Site Manager. Resolution target: 30 Jun 2026."
Change Control Governance
The formal process by which changes to the project's scope, schedule, or budget are requested, evaluated for impact, approved or rejected, and documented. No scope change is implemented without going through change control.
Why it matters: Without change control, scope creep is invisible until the budget is exhausted. Change control makes every modification a conscious, authorized decision.
Example: Client requests an additional floor be added mid-construction. A Change Request is raised, impact is assessed (cost +$200K, schedule +6 weeks), then approved or rejected by the Sponsor.
Procurement Executing
The structured process of obtaining goods, services, or works from an external source. Includes identifying needs, selecting vendors, negotiating contracts, managing performance, and closing agreements.
Why it matters: Procurement failures (wrong vendor, unclear contracts, poor performance monitoring) are one of the most common sources of cost overruns and schedule delays.
Example: Sourcing and contracting a subcontractor for structural steel installation — from RFQ/RFP through to contract execution and closeout.
Baseline Monitoring
The original formally approved version of the project plan — scope baseline, schedule baseline, and cost baseline. The Baseline is the fixed reference point against which all project performance is measured.
Why it matters: Without a baseline, you cannot measure variance. Every deviation in cost, time, or scope is measured against the baseline to determine if the project is on track.
Example: If the baseline schedule shows completion on 1 Dec 2026 and current forecast shows 15 Jan 2027, the project has a schedule variance of +45 days against baseline.
Project Charter Initiating
The foundational document that formally authorizes a project to exist. It defines the project's purpose, objectives, sponsor, stakeholders, constraints, assumptions, and high-level scope. No project should begin without one.
Why it matters: The Charter is your mandate. It aligns all parties on what the project is before a single dollar is spent. It is the document you return to when disputes arise about scope or intent.
Example: A signed Project Charter for a resort renovation stating objectives, authorized budget of $1.2M, completion date of Q3 2026, and names of the Sponsor and PM.
RFI — Request for Information Executing
A formal written question sent to the designer, engineer, or client when the drawings, specs, or scope are unclear or contradictory. An RFI pauses the affected work until a documented answer is received — it is not a casual clarification over the phone.
Why it matters: Undocumented "verbal clarifications" are how disputes are lost. An RFI creates a paper trail proving the field raised the issue and got a specific answer before proceeding.
Example: "RFI #14: Structural drawing S-102 shows a 200mm slab, architectural A-201 shows 250mm. Please confirm correct thickness before pour." Work on that slab holds until answered.
RFP — Request for Proposal Procurement
A formal solicitation issued to prospective vendors or subcontractors, describing the scope of work and asking them to submit a priced proposal, method statement, and timeline. Broader than a Request for Quote — an RFP asks vendors to propose an approach, not just a price.
Why it matters: A well-written RFP gets comparable bids. A vague one gets wildly different scopes disguised as different prices, which makes vendor selection unreliable.
Example: Issuing an RFP to three structural steel subcontractors, each required to respond with price, fabrication lead time, and site erection method.
Change Order / Variation Order Governance
The signed, priced instrument that formally executes an approved change to scope, cost, or schedule. Where "Change Control" is the process, a Change Order (or Variation Order, the common Commonwealth/Caribbean term) is the resulting contract document — it modifies the original contract sum and duration.
Why it matters: Extra work performed without a signed Change Order is extremely difficult to get paid for. Contractors who proceed on a verbal "go ahead" carry the financial risk themselves.
Example: Variation Order #3 adds a retaining wall not in the original drawings — contract sum increases by $45,000 and completion date extends by 9 days, both parties sign before work starts.
Punch List Closing
The itemized list of defects, omissions, and unfinished details identified during final inspection — the small items remaining before a deliverable is accepted as complete. Also called a "snag list" outside North America.
Why it matters: The Punch List is the gate to final payment and formal handover. An undisciplined punch list process is how projects drag on for months after the "real" work is done.
Example: Final walkthrough of a completed villa turns up 14 punch items — touch-up paint, a sticking door, missing light-switch cover plates — each assigned an owner and a close-out date.
Critical Path Scheduling
The longest sequence of dependent tasks through the schedule, from project start to finish, with zero slack — if any task on this path slips, the entire project finish date slips by the same amount. Tasks not on the critical path have float (buffer) and can move without affecting the deadline.
Why it matters: Not every delay is equal. A PM who protects the critical path and lets non-critical delays absorb into float is managing the schedule correctly; one who treats every slipped task as equally urgent is wasting attention.
Example: Foundation → Framing → Roofing → Final Inspection has zero float — it's critical. Landscaping can run any time before handover — it has float and isn't critical.
Earned Value Metrics — CPI & SPI Monitoring
Cost Performance Index (CPI) and Schedule Performance Index (SPI) measure efficiency, not just status. CPI = Earned Value ÷ Actual Cost — above 1.0 means you're getting more value than you're spending. SPI = Earned Value ÷ Planned Value — above 1.0 means you're ahead of schedule. Both are ratios, not dollar amounts.
Why it matters: "We're $50K over budget" tells you the size of a problem. A CPI of 0.82 tells you the rate of the problem — and lets you forecast where it ends if nothing changes. This app's Health Score uses CPI directly in the Budget factor.
Example: CPI of 0.90 means every dollar spent is only returning 90 cents of completed work — the project is trending toward a cost overrun even if today's total still looks manageable.
Contingency Reserve Risk Mgmt
Budget or time set aside in advance, specifically to absorb the cost or schedule impact of known risks already identified in the risk register. Distinct from Management Reserve, which covers unknown-unknowns and typically requires Sponsor approval to release.
Why it matters: A budget with no contingency isn't a realistic budget — it's a best-case fantasy. Naming the reserve explicitly (e.g. "8% contingency") protects it from being quietly absorbed into scope before risks even materialize.
Example: A $500K renovation budget carries a $40K (8%) contingency reserve specifically sized against the "unforeseen subsurface conditions" risk already logged in the risk register.
Work Package Planning
The lowest level of the WBS — a single group of related tasks small enough to be assigned to one owner, estimated, scheduled, and tracked as a unit. A Work Package is where planning turns into assignable, trackable work.
Why it matters: If a WBS branch is too broad to hand to one person with a clear deadline, it isn't a Work Package yet — it needs to be broken down further. This is the practical test for "is my WBS detailed enough?"
Example: Under "2.0 Electrical," the Work Package "2.3 Rough-in Wiring — Second Floor" is assigned to one electrical subcontractor with its own start date, duration, and completion criteria.
Lessons Learned Closing
The documented record of what worked, what didn't, and what should change next time — captured formally at project closure (and ideally at phase-end checkpoints too), not left as informal team memory that walks out the door with the team.
Why it matters: An organization that doesn't capture Lessons Learned re-learns the same expensive mistakes on every project. This is the single highest-leverage, lowest-cost improvement practice in project management — and the most commonly skipped.
Example: "Rebar deliveries from Supplier X were consistently 3–4 days late across two phases — source from an alternate supplier or order 1 week earlier on the next project" logged for the next PM to inherit.
Scrum Methodology
A specific, structured Agile framework built around fixed-length Sprints, a small cross-functional team, and defined ceremonies: Sprint Planning, a daily stand-up, Sprint Review, and Sprint Retrospective. Scrum is one concrete way to run Agile — not a synonym for it.
Why it matters: Teams often say "we're doing Agile" when they mean "we have no fixed process." Scrum's discipline — the ceremonies, the roles, the fixed Sprint length — is what actually makes an iterative approach work rather than collapse into chaos.
Example: A design-build interior fit-out team runs 2-week Sprints, with a 15-minute daily stand-up on site and a client walkthrough (Sprint Review) at the end of every Sprint.
Kanban Methodology
A continuous-flow methodology, distinct from Scrum's fixed Sprints, built around visualizing work as it moves through stages and enforcing Work-In-Progress (WIP) limits at each stage. There is no Sprint boundary — work is pulled into the next stage as capacity frees up, one item at a time.
Why it matters: Kanban suits steady-stream work with no natural batch boundary — punch-list closeout, maintenance requests, ongoing procurement — better than Scrum does, because there's no need to force it into artificial 2-week cycles.
Example: A facilities maintenance team limits "In Progress" to 4 tickets at a time — a 5th ticket can't start until one of the 4 moves to "Done," keeping the crew from being spread across too many jobs at once.
Lean Methodology
A methodology focused on maximizing value to the client while systematically eliminating waste — waiting, rework, overproduction, unnecessary movement, and excess inventory. Originated in manufacturing (Toyota Production System) and adapted to construction as Lean Construction.
Why it matters: Lean reframes the PM's job from "keep to the schedule" to "remove whatever is slowing the work down." On site, that often means redesigning material staging and trade sequencing more than pushing people to move faster.
Example: Re-sequencing material deliveries so rebar arrives just before it's needed instead of piling up on site for two weeks — less handling, less damage, less space consumed, same output.
Critical Path Method (CPM) Methodology
The scheduling technique — not just the resulting path — used to calculate the Critical Path. CPM works by mapping every task's dependencies, then running a forward pass (earliest possible start/finish) and a backward pass (latest allowable start/finish) to compute float for every task in the network.
Why it matters: CPM is what this app's Gantt engine runs automatically when it cascades dates from predecessors — this is the math underneath the "if this task slips, what else moves" feature, not a separate manual exercise.
Example: CPM calculates that "Order Windows" has 12 days of float (it can start up to 12 days late without delaying the project) while "Pour Foundation" has zero float and must start on schedule.
RACI Matrix Governance
An accountability grid that assigns exactly one of four roles to each person on each task or deliverable: Responsible (does the work), Accountable (owns the outcome — should be exactly one person per task), Consulted (two-way input before the work happens), Informed (one-way, kept in the loop after).
Why it matters: the RACI tab pulls its rows and columns directly from your live WBS tasks and Resources/Stakeholders, so it never drifts out of sync with the actual project. Click a cell to cycle R → A → C → I → blank; right-click to go back a step. The app automatically flags any task with no Accountable person, and any person Accountable for more than 5 tasks.
Example: on "Pour Foundation," the Site Supervisor is Accountable, the Concrete Crew is Responsible, the Structural Engineer is Consulted before the pour, and the Client is Informed once it's done.
Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) Planning
A hierarchical decomposition of the total project scope into progressively smaller, manageable pieces — from major phases down to individual work packages — each with its own WBS code (1, 1.1, 1.1.1...) showing exactly where it sits in the hierarchy.
Why it matters: the WBS is the single source of truth this entire app is built on top of. Every task you create here is what drives the Gantt chart, the RACI matrix, the Budget lines, EVM, the Health Score, and the Timeline Target Tracker — nothing else in the app has its own separate task list.
Example: 1.0 Foundation → 1.1 Excavation → 1.1.1 Site Survey, 1.1.2 Soil Testing — each level nests inside the one above it.
Gantt Chart Scheduling
A horizontal bar chart that plots every task against a timeline, showing start date, duration, and finish date as a single bar, with dependency arrows connecting predecessor tasks to the tasks that follow them.
Why it matters: in this app the Gantt is a live view of the same WBS task list, not a separate model to keep updated — move a task's dates here and CPM recalculates float and the critical path immediately.
Example: "Pour Foundation" runs as a 5-day bar from Aug 3–7; "Frame Walls" starts right after on Aug 8, connected by a dependency arrow.
Monte Carlo Simulation Methodology
A forecasting technique that runs a schedule (or cost) through hundreds or thousands of randomized "what if" trials — each task's duration is sampled from a realistic best-case/most-likely/worst-case range — and reports the spread of possible finish dates as probabilities (e.g. an 80% chance of finishing by a given date).
Why it matters: a single "most likely" finish date hides how much risk is baked into a schedule. The Monte Carlo simulator in this app uses your task durations and the risk factor/adder you set to show a realistic range instead of one falsely-precise date.
Example: instead of "the project finishes March 15," Monte Carlo reports "70% chance of finishing by March 20, 90% chance by March 28."
DMAIC Methodology
A five-phase Six Sigma process-improvement cycle: Define the problem, Measure current performance, Analyze root causes, Improve by implementing a fix, Control to make sure the fix sticks.
Why it matters: DMAIC is the structured way to work a recurring problem (a defect rate, a repeated schedule slip cause, a quality issue) that a one-off Change Request isn't built to handle — it's a cycle, not a single ask.
Example: Define — window installs keep failing inspection. Measure — 18% first-time failure rate. Analyze — root cause is inconsistent flashing technique. Improve — retrain crew + add a checklist. Control — spot-check next 20 installs.
Timeline Target Variance Monitoring
The gap, in days, between the Target Completion Date set in the Charter (the date the Sponsor signed off on, before a single task may even have been scheduled) and the Current Planned Finish — the latest end date among all currently-scheduled tasks.
Why it matters: this is a deliberately different question from EVM's Schedule Performance Index (which measures efficiency of work performed vs. planned) and from Baseline Variance (which measures drift from a saved snapshot). Timeline Target Variance answers, in one glance: "if every task finishes on the date currently typed into it, do we still land inside the date we promised?" It's tracked on the Dashboard's Timeline Target Tracker card and surfaces as a badge in the header once a project slips past its target.
Example: Target Completion is Feb 28; the latest task currently scheduled ends March 14 — that's a 14-day Timeline Target Variance, flagged "At Risk."
